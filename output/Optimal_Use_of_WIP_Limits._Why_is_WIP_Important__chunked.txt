Optimal Use of WIP Limits Why is WIP Important?
==================================================
Chunk 4:
Work-In-progress (WIP)is a concept that
originated in the Lean manufacturing movement.
==================================================
Chunk 5:
In both a financial and operational sense
WIP is considered a form of waste: work that has been done and paid for but is providing
no benefit to the customer or the company.
==================================================
Chunk 6:
WIP is an even more insidious problem in a
software development context. In addition to financial and operational complications WIP
imposes an additional tax on a software engineering team in the form of increased
complexity and cognitive load.
==================================================
Chunk 7:
WIP in Manufacturing The ideal process in both
manufacturing and software is often single-piece flow.
==================================================
Chunk 8:
In single-piece flow once work
begins it continues without interruption until value is delivered to a customer.
==================================================
Chunk 9:
WIP is
reduced to its theoretical minimal level with single-piece flow.
==================================================
Chunk 10:
On the other hand,
consider what happens in the absence of single-piece flow.
==================================================
Chunk 11:
Some value-adding work has been
done on a product — but then work has to stop for a while before it continues and the
product is finished.
==================================================
Chunk 12:
Maybe required materials are not present and the product must be
stored until those materials arrive.
==================================================
Chunk 13:
Perhaps the product needs to be moved to another
location before value-adding work can begin again — the time and expense involved in
moving the product is a form of waste.
==================================================
Chunk 14:
Partially finished items need to be kept track of,
moved and stored until they can be completed and delivered to customers.
==================================================
Chunk 15:
The extra effort
to do this provides no value to the customer or the company and is wasteful.
==================================================
Chunk 16:
WIP in
Software Development The issues with partially finished work are similar in software
development.
==================================================
Chunk 17:
In this context WIP is source code or other resources that have been created
but are not yet in production producing value for users. In most cases WIP takes the form
of completed code that is not yet in production.
==================================================
Chunk 18:
It can be code on an engineer’s laptop
that is not committed to a repository, code committed to a repository but not yet in a
DevOps pipeline, or code in a DevOps pipeline waiting to be deployed.
==================================================
Chunk 19:
Either way it is
waste: something that time and money has been spent on that is not producing value for the
customer.
==================================================
Chunk 20:
A software development team must spend precious cognitive bandwidth managing
WIP.
==================================================
Chunk 21:
This takes effort and reduces the amount of time and energy that can be spent
delivering features that provide value to the customer.
==================================================
Chunk 22:
In software development, as in
manufacturing, it is very important to keep WIP at a minimum.
==================================================
Chunk 23:
Cognitive load is a concept
introduced in 1988 by psychologist John Sweller and recently made more popular by the book
Team Topologies: Organizing Business and Technology Teams for Fast Flow by Matthew Skepton
and Manuel Pais.
==================================================
Chunk 24:
In software engineering there are three main ways cognitive load or
mental capacity can be spent: 1) needless tasks that provide limited value such as manual
deployments or paperwork, 2) well-known foundational elements of the current task like
knowledge of a particular programming language or commonly used libraries, or 3) higher
level “value add” thinking about architectural concerns or code-level tactical decisions
like API design.
==================================================
Chunk 25:
A team’s capacity to handle cognitive load is very limited.
==================================================
Chunk 26:
Great care
must be taken to ensure the most time is spent on higher level “value add” thinking.
==================================================
Chunk 27:
A
high amount of WIP often means engineers are forced to work on more than one item at a
time.
==================================================
Chunk 28:
Often a great many items at the same time.
==================================================
Chunk 29:
Paying attention to all these tasks
increases the cognitive load on both individuals and the overall team while increasing the
complexity of the tasks the team must handle.
==================================================
Chunk 30:
Excessive WIP can cause the performance and
morale of a team to deteriorate badly.
==================================================
Chunk 31:
On the other hand, low WIP implies each engineer
can focus on just a few ( or ideally, one) thing at a time.
==================================================
Chunk 32:
The ideal state is when an
engineer begins a task they have everything needed to complete that task and can work
uninterrupted until that task is completed.
==================================================
Chunk 33:
There is often a small pause until that code
passes code review, at which point it enters a build pipeline and again continues without
interruption until it is released into production.
==================================================
Chunk 34:
This seamless and constant flow of code
reduces cognitive load on individuals and the overall team.
==================================================
Chunk 35:
Limiting WIP shortens the lead
time for new features, reduces context switching, and allows engineers to concentrate
resources and attention on the most important tasks.
==================================================
Chunk 36:
When engineers focus on only one
thing at a time they are more productive and generally more satisfied with their work.
==================================================
Chunk 37:
They are more likely to stay with you.
==================================================
Chunk 38:
What happens if automated testing and deployment of
code changes is not in place?
==================================================
Chunk 39:
Then that code needs to be stored somewhere (often long
running release branches) while waiting for approval.
==================================================
Chunk 40:
The team needs to keep track of what
features are in what branch and be aware of any interactions between branches.
==================================================
Chunk 41:
In
practice, there are often quite a few interactions between long running branches which
makes testing difficult.
==================================================
Chunk 42:
The context switching involved in this approach imposes a high
cognitive load on a team and greatly reduces their capacity to provide value to the
customer and company.
==================================================
Chunk 43:
The Wrong Branching Model Can Increase WIP Gitflow vs Trunk-based
Development Many software engineering teams use long-running release branches, sometimes
multiple release branches.
==================================================
Chunk 44:
In this approach releases are infrequent and the idea is to
work on multiple features at once and have them scheduled for multiple release dates.
==================================================
Chunk 45:
There may be valid business reasons this approach is used, and if that is the case then
not much can be done.
==================================================
Chunk 46:
However, most often long-running release branches are in place
simply because a team does not know how to deliver more frequent, smaller releases.
==================================================
Chunk 47:
By
definition, each of these long-running release branches contains WIP. By definition each
release branch is WIP.
==================================================
Chunk 48:
Engineers have to keep track of what features are in each release
branch while also developing new features.
==================================================
Chunk 49:
This sort of structure creates distraction and
requires frequent context switching.
==================================================
Chunk 50:
It can be very error-prone.
==================================================
Chunk 51:
Using the right branching
strategy is critical to minimizing WIP.
==================================================
Chunk 52:
Branching models like Gitflow
(https://nvie.com/posts/a-successful-git-branching-model/) split the code base into
several long running branches, each designated for a future release.
==================================================
Chunk 53:
The cognitive load on
a team using Gitflow tends to be high.
==================================================
Chunk 54:
They have to remember what each branch is for, what
features are included in each branch, and how code in each branch interacts with other
branches.
==================================================
Chunk 55:
There are many checks as code winds its way to production.
==================================================
Chunk 56:
Gitflow is the right
tool for certain use cases, but the payment for all the protection it offers are very
large, infrequent releases and large amounts of WIP.
==================================================
Chunk 57:
Gitflow: image by Vincent Driessen
Trunk-based Development: image by Toptal To reduce WIP (and increase deployment
frequency/decrease deployment size), take a look at Trunk-based development
(https://trunkbaseddevelopment.com/).
==================================================
Chunk 58:
Trunk-based development is a practice where
developers merge small, frequent updates to a core main branch.
==================================================
Chunk 59:
Trunk-based development
has strong support in the DevOps community as it streamlines merging, reduces WIP and
reduces cognitive load.
==================================================
Chunk 60:
How WIP Limits are Used Many teams try to use WIP Limits as a way
of reducing WIP and the waste and high cognitive load a high level of WIP places on a
team. By focusing on an increasingly smaller number of tasks (the theoretical ideal WIP
Limit is 1), WIP Limits are used to reduce rework and duplicative efforts, eliminate the
context switching that comes with juggling multiple tasks, cut down on unnecessary
meetings, improve communications and eliminate handoffs between teams or parts of teams.
==================================================
Chunk 61:
What is a WIP Limit? A WIP Limit is simply a limit on the number of unfinished tasks an
engineer or team can work on at the same time. The optimal WIP limit is 1 — meaning an
engineer works on only one task until that task is completed.
==================================================
Chunk 62:
Once the task is completed
the engineer switches to the next task.
==================================================
Chunk 63:
The goal is to minimize (and ultimately prevent)
multitasking and the context switching that happens each time an engineer stops working on
one task and picks up another.
==================================================
Chunk 64:
In many cases the rule is that if a WIP Limit is reached
engineers perform non-coding tasks like code reviews, training, etc.
==================================================
Chunk 65:
Reducing WIP to a
bare minimum is an important goal in any DevOps or other workflow transformation.
==================================================
Chunk 66:
However,
WIP Limits need to be used very carefully or they can backfire. This article provides a
quick background on the concept WIP Limits as well as advice on how to best use WIP
Limits.
==================================================
Chunk 67:
As discussed, the optimal WIP Limit is one.
==================================================
Chunk 68:
In an ideal workflow, an engineer
would work on one item until it is completed and passed off to an automated CI/CD pipeline
that would then test and deploy that code.
==================================================
Chunk 69:
The engineer would then immediately begin work
on the next item with no delay.
==================================================
Chunk 70:
In this scenario the engineer begins the task with
everything needed to complete their task without interruption (the exception often being a
little time spent waiting for code reviews).
==================================================
Chunk 71:
Using WIP Limits for a Scrum Team The
illustration below shows a simple and hypothetical development team scrum board.
==================================================
Chunk 72:
Stories

==================================================
Chunk 73:
Example

==================================================
Chunk 74:
WIP Limits deal with items in the “In

worked.
==================================================
Chunk 75:
On some teams WIP Limits apply to the entire team, while other teams apply WIP
Limits to each individual.
==================================================
Chunk 76:
In either case the objective is to reduce the amount of work-
in-progress and all the negative effects discussed above.
==================================================
Chunk 77:
Where WIP Limits Work — and
Where They Don’t WIP Limits are often triggered when the entire engineering work stream is
not set up for optimal flow.
==================================================
Chunk 78:
The typical symptom of non-optimal flow is when work on a
story is partially completed, but then work has to stop while the engineer waits for
clarification on requirements, waits for some approval to proceed further, or has to drop
their work to focus on an important production defect.
==================================================
Chunk 79:
If there is a WIP Limit of 1 then
that blocked engineer is idle — they can’t work on anything else until the blockage is
cleared.
==================================================
Chunk 80:
Idle engineers are obviously wasteful and few teams in this situation end up
using WIP Limits for long, even if the idle time is used for training or other productive
tasks. An increase in idle engineers often ends up being the fatal flaw in using WIP
Limits to spur process improvements. Idle engineers are usually only tolerated if: A
company is willing to let engineers be idle for possibly lengthy periods of time until the
workflow is improved to the point where WIP Limits are not triggered.
==================================================
Chunk 81:
The engineering team
using WIP Limits controls the parts of the process that need improvement.
==================================================
Chunk 82:
If the delays
triggering a WIP Limit are the result of another department (possibly QA or Operations)
then the fact your team has reached a WIP Limit just may not matter to them.
==================================================
Chunk 83:
It’s possible
these other groups might not be motivated to improve their pieces of the process so your
team’s WIP Limits are not triggered.
==================================================
Chunk 84:
Despite what anyone might say, most companies are not
going to tolerate idle engineers for long.
==================================================
Chunk 85:
When the head of engineering complains their
engineers are idle due to WIP Limits triggered because testing is not automated, the first
question the group VP will ask is “Why can’t the engineers work on something else while
they wait?” Be careful how you answer this question.
==================================================
Chunk 86:
Arguing that WIP Limits are good
because they reduce cognitive load and stress might not play well with stakeholders.
==================================================
Chunk 87:
One
can always reduce cognitive load and stress simply by being idle.
==================================================
Chunk 88:
Instead the goal is to
accomplish quite a bit while keeping cognitive load and stress in check.
==================================================
Chunk 89:
You don’t want to
be on the wrong side of this argument.
==================================================
Chunk 90:
Leading a Transformation If you are leading an
agile or devops transformation (or the second, third or fourth attempt at a
transformation) you need to be aware of organizational history, internal politics and
manage how your team presents itself across the company.
==================================================
Chunk 91:
If past transformations have
failed or only been partially successful the organization might be jaded on the prospect
of any improvement.
==================================================
Chunk 92:
There is likely a long backlog of engineering work the business would
have liked to be completed long ago.
==================================================
Chunk 93:
Stakeholders want engineers working to reduce that
backlog.
==================================================
Chunk 94:
When stakeholders learn WIP Limits are reducing the amount of time engineers
spend coding (even if only temporarily), precious political capital will need to be spent
to keep them supporting the transformation.
==================================================
Chunk 95:
Why incur that expense?
==================================================
Chunk 96:
You’ll need that
political capital later, maybe if your team falls behind schedule or the first time you
release a serious defect to production.
==================================================
Chunk 97:
Nothing ever goes as smoothly as you think it
will.
==================================================
Chunk 98:
We have found it more effective and less politically risky to challenge a team to
improve DORA devops metrics instead of implementing WIP Limits.
==================================================
Chunk 99:
Focusing on improving
Deployment Frequency coupled with Change Failure Rate is often a good place to start.
==================================================
Chunk 100:
If
your team is currently deploying once per month, challenge them to deploy every two weeks,
then weekly, then at will.
==================================================
Chunk 101:
And do that without increasing the number of defects found in
production.
==================================================
Chunk 102:
From the DORA 2021 Report: Improving DORA metrics is a tangible task the team
can focus on immediately.
==================================================
Chunk 103:
It will provide tangible benefits to the company.
==================================================
Chunk 104:
Tools like
Value-Stream Mapping can be used to eliminate waste in any process.
==================================================
Chunk 105:
Remember, optimized
processes minimize WIP.
==================================================
Chunk 106:
Accomplish just this and you’ll be making a substantial
contribution to the good of the company.
==================================================
Chunk 107:
The exception to this rule is if your team does
not control the entire process.
==================================================
Chunk 108:
Maybe they are assigned to help another team by working on
backlog items and they don’t have any control or input into Deployment Frequency.
==================================================
Chunk 109:
In this
case WIP Limits might be helpful, at least as a tool to help other teams understand they
need to improve their processes.
==================================================
Chunk 110:
The Canary in the Coal Mine WIP Limits do have one great
advantage.
==================================================
Chunk 111:
A triggered WIP Limit is often the first indication a high performing
engineering team is beginning to backslide. If engineers start hitting WIP limits it means
your DORA metrics are soon to decline and you have a problem to solve.
==================================================
Chunk 112:
A good approach is
to set a WIP Limit just above what the normal WIP level is.
==================================================
Chunk 113:
In this sense WIP Limits are
like the proverbial canary in the coal mine — an early warning system.
==================================================
Chunk 114:
To summarize, if
you set a WIP Limit before flow is optimized you are bound to have engineers spend
substantial time on non-engineering tasks.
==================================================
Chunk 115:
No one likes that.
==================================================
Chunk 116:
Engineers don’t like that
and your business stakeholders surely won’t like that.
==================================================
Chunk 117:
You can argue the slack time
produced by WIP Limits will be spent on other important tasks like training.
==================================================
Chunk 118:
That may be
true but we think this is a bad way to manage important tasks.
==================================================
Chunk 119:
Your team will never know
when the WIP Limit will be triggered and when they can spend time training, or when WIP
will drop under the WIP Limit and they have to get back to engineering work.
==================================================
Chunk 120:
This seems
like a haphazard approach to something like training, which is an important part of your
team’s professional development.

Building Software that Thrives on Change In Kenzan’s
experience engineering organizations can be broken down into three groups: Base-
levelengineering organizations employ many manual processes.
==================================================
Chunk 122:
Performance declines rapidly
under a limited amount of change or load.
==================================================
Chunk 123:
Their capacity for improvement is small and they
can only recover to baseline performance with extreme difficulty.
==================================================
Chunk 124:
Effectiveengineering
organizations produce good quality software while enduring a moderate amount of change or
load.
==================================================
Chunk 125:
If stress exceeds a certain threshold performance rapidly declines.
==================================================
Chunk 126:
These
organizations use some automated processes while still depending on manual work for
important tasks.
==================================================
Chunk 127:
Their capacity for improvement is modest and they can recover to baseline
performance with moderate difficulty.
==================================================
Chunk 128:
Eliteengineering organizations grow stronger the
more change or load is applied to them, in the same way the human body grows stronger
through rigorous exercise.
==================================================
Chunk 129:
They actively seek to learn from mistakes and incorporate those
learnings into future work, often embracing cloud infrastructure and cloud-native
architectures.
==================================================
Chunk 130:
These organizations depend heavily on automation to increase quality while
reducing cognitive load on teams and individuals.
==================================================
Chunk 131:
Their capacity for improvement is great
and under stress they can recover to baseline performance with relative ease.
==================================================

Getting
Agile and DevOps transformations right is critical to becoming an elite engineering
organization.
